# 18 September 2026 — The sheep harness and the collie references come out

The hosted-rc experiment (sheep's collie) was shut down, and running an
agent off the laptop is expected back in another form. So that the next
form is not read through the old one's names, the sheep harness and every
mention of the collie were removed from the code, the tests and the agent
guide, rather than left dormant.

**What went.** `packages/rc/src/sheep.ts` and `packages/cli/src/sheep.ts`,
with their six test files and the fake `sheep` binary. `--harness sheep` is
no longer a harness `isocan harness` finds or `isocan rc add` names. From
the room module: `RoomDeps.endSession` and `whereOf`, `RoomAdapter.place`,
`where` and `bornPass`, and the two extra arguments to
`RoomRows.setSessionId` — each existed for a session that outlives the
process, and the sheep was the only one. From the rc row: `sheep` and
`cellPass`. From the CLI: the cell's badge in `isocan badges`, the cell's
problem line in `isocan who`, the `cell` fence state, the sheep half of
both `remove` verbs, and the station host as a bench row's `runsAt`.
`isocan pass --agent` stays, and its printed hint no longer names
`collie new --pass`.

**What stayed, on purpose.** Everything the experiment left that is
generic: `DaemonRoutes` and the address helpers exported from `isocan/rc`,
`POST /api/rc/release`, the home-asked liveness of #306, and
`GET /api/projects/:id/passes/:passId`. That last route now has no caller
in the CLI; it is a deployed wire route and a minter's read of its own
pass, so it was left rather than removed in the same change.

**What was not touched.** The changelog, the closed `sheep-harness`
project and the research notes are records and read as they did; the four
that claimed something stands (`sheep-harness`, *Sheep as standing agents*,
*A sheep on two machines*, *The bearer is mostly built*) are now
`superseded` in their front matter, which is what the roadmap reads. The
design-partner baseline keeps `sheep.ts.txt`: it is a hashed snapshot of
source at a pinned revision, and the eval refuses a baseline whose hashes
moved. The farm theme's sheep cursor (#195) and the *sheepdog* mention-gate
design are different things and stay.

**A row that already says sheep.** An `rc-agents.json` row with
`"harness": "sheep"` is now an unknown harness: a summons for it fails with
the ordinary *no ACP adapter for harness "sheep"* line. `isocan rc add
<name> --harness <other>` re-enrols it; the row's old `sheep` and
`cellPass` fields are ignored. Nothing ends the sheep or the cell's badge
that such a row left behind — `sheep` and `isocan badges --kill` do that by
hand.

## The first minute is designed (#332)

An agent in a small hosted sandbox spends most of a person's minute on
isocan itself: 35 s installing 227 packages, 2 to 4 s starting the CLI for
every command, about 50k tokens of guide. A laptop hides all of it, so it
was measured rather than argued. `isocan --version` loads 456 modules; 297
are our own `.ts` files, 4.7 MB, transpiled or cache-checked by tsx on every
command, and all of `@isocan/server` and every module's guide load whatever
the command is. An esbuild bundle of the same CLI, built as an experiment and
thrown away, starts in 0.17 s against 0.29 s warm and 0.38 s cold. The
`release` branch is about 40 MB, of which `docs/` is 14 MB that no install
runs.

[`docs/projects/first-minute/`](../projects/first-minute/) is the project:
three journeys with a number each, the measurements and the changes (a
bundled release CLI, an install that resolves nothing, lazy loading, a guide
in tiers, a summons that carries its thread, an identity for an agent that
holds no secret), and a seven-phase walk. Nothing is built. Three decisions
are left open for Dimitri: a thin agent-only artifact, how the guide is cut,
and the shape of the no-secret identity.

## The room tells a host who a turn is for, and lets it hold one (#333)

Two small additions to `isocan/rc`, the first things #333 asks for that
wait on no decision. #333 itself narrowed the same day, in its comments: a
hosted place for agents is not a new party at the desk. A space at the host
is a machine of the person's that somebody else runs, joined by a pass they
mint, as a laptop is.

**`RoomTurn` carries `canvasId`, `agent` and `owner`.** The laptop reads
those from `cwd`, the environment and `isocan whoami`. A host that serves
many people has none of the three, and files its records and counts its
allowance by them. Additive; the laptop's adapter ignores them.

**`RoomHold`.** Anything a host threw from `adapterFor` or `open` was a
failed turn: the thread read "couldn't answer … `isocan rc`'s log has the
detail", and the room tried again every minute. For a spent allowance that
is the wrong sentence, about a log the person cannot read, repeated until
the reset. A host now throws `new RoomHold(line, retryAfter)`: the room says
`line` in the thread in the system voice, once, puts up no face, keeps the
summons pending and asks again at `retryAfter`, as it already does for the
guard's hourly ceiling. A held turn still counts as one of the hour's turns,
as a failed one does.

**Read, not changed: what ending a space's badge ends.** `killAndSweep`
expels every badge the dead badge passed onto a canvas, so ending a space
from the identity menu ends her sandbox badge there too
(`sweep.test.ts`, "ends the holder and sweeps every room"). With one
exception that is by design for machines and open for agents: where a link
grant is live on the canvas the sweep re-roots her badge onto the link
("does not un-invite"), and she stays admitted under her own name, at the
link's rung. Her badge claims only her, so it is not one of the person's
surfaces and they cannot end it themselves. Reported on #333; no fix chosen.
## The first minute, phases 0 and 1: the numbers, and a bundled release CLI

The design above was argued from somebody else's sandbox, so the first thing
built was a way to re-take its numbers. `node scripts/first-minute.mjs`
installs today's `release` into a container with four cores, Node 22, a cold
disk and no tsx cache, and prints what an agent waits for: install 14.2 s for
227 packages and 115 MB on disk with `docs/` included, `isocan --version`
1.12 s cold and 1.07 s warm where `node -e ''` is 0.04 s, and **4397 `openat`
calls for that one command, of which only 1396 found a file**. The container
reproduces seconds-per-command but not #332's 3.3 s, and gVisor was not there
to close the gap, so the file count is the number the later phases are held
to: 3001 opens that found nothing is a resolver walking directories that do
not exist, and a sandbox that intercepts the file system charges for every
one.

Then the CLI stopped being sources. `scripts/release.mjs` now builds
`packages/cli/dist/isocan.mjs` with esbuild and the release manifest's `bin`
names it, so an installed copy starts without tsx and without opening 297
`.ts` files. A checkout is unchanged and still runs `bin/isocan.js`.

**It is split into chunks rather than one file, and the measurement is why.**
Bundled into a single output `--version` loaded 538 modules — more than
source mode's 437 — because esbuild hoists an inlined module's external
imports to the top of the file it lands in, so every
`await import("./design-system.ts")` in the CLI dragged fastify, the MCP SDK,
ajv and the remark stack into startup. With `splitting` each dynamic import
keeps its own chunk: 142 modules and 0.20 s against 437 and 0.38 s. The
laziness the source already had is worth keeping, and phase 3's work now
counts instead of cancelling out.

**What a bundle broke, and the one helper that fixes it.** Six files found
their way about by counting directories up from `import.meta.url` — the CLI's
`myRoot()`, the server's build stamp and the web app's `dist`, the daemon bin
the API spawns. In a bundle every folded-in file shares one `import.meta.url`,
so counting cannot work. `packageRoot()` in `@isocan/core/packageroot` walks
up to the `package.json` named `isocan` instead: one answer from a checkout,
the bundle, `npm i -g`'s tree and `~/.isocan/current/node_modules/isocan`. It
is a subpath rather than part of core's index because the index is bundled for
browsers, where a `node:fs` import fails the build. `packageBin()` beside it
reads a tree's own manifest, because `daemonBin()` used to name
`packages/cli/bin/isocan.js` and a bundled install has no such file.

**The guides are imported, not read.** `import guide from "./agent-guide.md"`
is that file's text — one rule with four adapters (the source-mode loader, the
two esbuild builds, the suite), declared once in `md.d.ts`. Two things the
design had not named turned up: `@isocan/cloudstore` has to be declared
external, because esbuild follows the static `import()` specifier that exists
precisely to keep its 156 packages out of a CLI install, and the first bundle
built here carried @google-cloud/firestore.

`test/cli-bundle.test.ts` holds both halves: the bundle starts from a tree
with no `.ts` in it and answers `--version`, `--help`, `--agent-help` and a
module verb, and `--version` loads fewer than 150 modules with no tsx among
them. It was 456 when the project started; phase 3 takes it under 40.

Measured in phase 0's sandbox against a `release` built from the bundle:
**`isocan --version` takes 0.14 s where it took 1.12 s, and opens 474 files
where it opened 4397.** That is past journey 1's 0.5 s target before the lazy
loading of phase 3 has started. The install has not moved — the same 227
packages, 10 s — which is exactly what phase 2 is for.

## The first minute, phase 2: the install resolves nothing

`npm install -g github:dglazkov/isocan#release` took 14.2 s in phase 0's
sandbox and installed 227 packages and 115 MB. It now takes **3.9 s and
installs three packages and 22 MB** — and none of the three is code: every
dependency is inside the bundle, and `@types/node` is there so an editor can
read what `connect()` returns.

`RELEASE_DEPENDENCIES` is the list of survivors with the reason each one
survived, and the build fails on a name main does not declare. `RELEASE_DROPS`
is what leaves the branch: `docs/` (15 MB nobody runs), `test/`, the sources
themselves — after the bundling nothing resolves them, so a `.ts` file on the
release branch would be a second copy of the CLI that can disagree with the
one that runs — the web app's build inputs, this repository's own workings,
the tool configuration, and `package-lock.json`, which listed 227 packages the
manifest no longer asks for. Of `scripts/`, three files survive: the two a CLI
verb spawns and the browser helper they share; the other forty import a
package's `src`. 40 MB became 17.

Two things only running it could find. Half the dependency tree is CommonJS,
and esbuild's `require` shim needs a real `require` to fall back to — the
first fully-inlined bundle died on its first command with *Dynamic require of
"node:events" is not supported*, which a `createRequire` banner on each chunk
answers. And `index.mjs` and `rc.mjs` — `import { connect } from "isocan"` —
registered tsx and imported the API from source, so dropping the sources would
have broken the package's own module entries; they are built in the same
esbuild pass as the CLI, sharing its chunks, and the release commits one
re-export line for each.

`test/cli-bundle.test.ts` now starts a daemon from a tree with no
`node_modules` in it at all and asks it for `/healthz` and the page.

## The first minute, phase 3: a barrel that meant "and also fastify"

Phase 3 was to move the server, the MCP layer and the design stack behind
`import()`. Both of its proofs were already green when it opened — splitting
the bundle and inlining the dependencies had taken `isocan --version` to 33
modules and 0.12 s, under the "40 modules" and "0.5 s" bars — and the module
count had stopped meaning much, because once every dependency is inside the
bundle a chunk is one module however much is in it.

So the phase was re-aimed at the number that still moves, **bytes read at
startup**, and the first look at those found what the design had not:
`@isocan/server`'s index re-exported `startDaemon`, and sixteen files in the
CLI and the API import that index for `paths` or `readConfigFile`. Printing a
version string read a 2.1 MB chunk of fastify and its plugins. The daemon is
now `@isocan/server/daemon`, reached with `await import()` by the one verb
that serves; its types stay in the index, because a type import is erased and
costs nothing. 5.8 MB became 4.5, and 0.12 s became 0.10.

The rest of the list was left alone on purpose: moving zod, `ws` and undici
out of startup would be a large change through the CLI's import graph for
perhaps 2 MB, and the targets are met with five times the margin. A
startup-bytes budget now sits in `test/cli-bundle.test.ts` so the next person
to add a static import to a barrel finds out on their own laptop, where the
whole command is a tenth of a second and nothing else would tell them.

Across the three phases, in phase 0's sandbox: **install 14.2 s → 4.1 s, 227
packages → 3, `isocan --version` 1.12 s → 0.10 s, and 4397 file opens → 49.**

## A stranded claim, and the wrong remedy printed beside it

The `roadmap-sync` timer had been failing for eight days. Its canvas, Roadmap
Test, was frozen at `434c9198` (10 Sep) while `origin/main` had moved 98
commits past it. The reason was one line in the service's own stderr:

    no actor is claimed under session "cron:roadmap-sync" — claim it once:
    ISOCAN_HARNESS=cron ISOCAN_SESSION_ID=roadmap-sync isocan identity --name "Your Name" --session

The actor *was* claimed. `Roadmap Sync` (`usr_AgRvjzUbIN`) had been claimed
under that exact key on 10 September — and the claim was still on the desk,
held by `bdg_f9YL8cthAI`. This machine re-badged on 12 September; the live
badge `bdg_ep0NQZDaZC` held five claims and not that one, and the migration
shelf was empty, so there was no legacy row left to adopt. The claim had not
been lost. It had been stranded on a badge nobody holds.

That is the case `noIdentityHere` was written for, and it says so plainly: the
identity IS configured, it is just not reachable from here, and `--name` would
mint a stranger wearing the same name and leave the history behind — "the
precise mistake `--as` exists to prevent". The ambient walk got that answer.
A script stating its own identity did not, because `connect()` printed a fixed
gesture and never asked the desk anything. So the timer's error advised the one
recovery that would have made the damage permanent: a second `Roadmap Sync`,
refused as name-taken, or admitted and splitting every later version and every
`undo` across two actors.

`connect()` asks now. The explicit-identity refusal looks up
`/api/actors/orphaned` for the one key the caller stated — key-scoped, so the
answer can never be a roster — and when that key is claimed on a badge this
machine no longer holds, it names the actor and prints `--as <actorId>` instead
of `--name`. A home that cannot answer degrades to the old generic gesture
rather than failing the refusal. The helper is private to `connect.ts` on
purpose: the API ceiling in `test/entry.test.ts` counts names a caller outside
the package needs, and this one has exactly one caller, inside it. Nothing
asserted the old message, and the new test is red without the fix — it
reproduces the eight-day stderr verbatim.

The recovery itself was the daemon's own sentence, run once:

    ISOCAN_HARNESS=cron ISOCAN_SESSION_ID=roadmap-sync isocan identity --session --as usr_AgRvjzUbIN

The timer's next scheduled fire, eight seconds later, did the rest: 109
operations in 90 seconds, every one of them stamped `Roadmap Sync`
(`usr_AgRvjzUbIN`) and none anonymous, 56 documents rewritten and 30 cards
added, the canvas caught up to `a770255c`. Checked against the repository
rather than against the script's own log: all 103 documents the current
`docs/ROADMAP.md` links now match `origin/main` byte for byte, by blob hash.
One card did not move and should not have — `docs/projects/memory/design.md`
left the roadmap table at `b5755f08`, and a file that leaves the roadmap
leaves its card behind, because nothing here deletes. The fire after that ran
quiet in 1.5 seconds: 103 tracked, 0 changed, exit 0.

The wider lesson is the part worth keeping. A refusal that names a remedy is
only as good as the remedy, and this one was confidently wrong for the single
case most likely to reach it — a long-running script whose badge outlived
itself. The desk already knew the truth; the door just was not asking.

## The WebMCP co-browsing transport is designed, from an executed census

The September 11 registration described an MCP vocabulary of six read tools and
a browser API named `navigator.modelContext`, and said the write half waited on
addressability. All three were stale, so the design re-ran the census before
arguing from it: a real stdio handshake against the shipped server answered
**sixteen** tools — re-counted behaviourally in the corrected census as **eleven
non-mutating and five mutation/claim** (the earlier ten/six split was a grouping
of the inventory, not behaviour, and this entry had it both ways until 18 Sep) — and the
browser API was measured on two Chromes rather than read — `document.modelContext`
is present by default on Chrome for Testing 150.0.7871.24 and behind
`--enable-features=WebMCP` on stable 152.0.7977.82, `provideContext()` is gone,
and the spec now carries four annotation hints where the registration said there
were none. Two behaviours phase 1 must carry came out of running it, not reading
it: `executeTool` rejects an object argument (`Failed to parse input arguments`)
and accepts a JSON string, and that `getTools()` retains `readOnlyHint` and
`untrustedContentHint` while omitting `consequentialHint` and `debugging` even
when they are set true. The projection behaviour was observed; the rule behind
it is undetermined, and no phase rests on the hint. An earlier revision of this
entry claimed `getTools()` "elides false annotation hints" — it does not, and
that claim is superseded here.

`docs/projects/webmcp-cobrowsing/design.md` argues the transport: the sixteen
tools moved into the page the human is already on, the MCP identity behaviour as
actually measured (an unclaimed ambient session may write; a claimed session is
a distinct actor; a missing explicit session is refused — whether the write half
is separately addressable or undoable is *not* established by that measurement
and is left open), deixis taken from the live selection on the ephemeral plane,
every authored text marked `untrustedContentHint`, canvas writes simply not
returned as consequential in the projection (the reason is undetermined, and no
phase claims one), and graceful absence where the API is not enabled. `phases.md` is the six-phase walk, each phase with the
browser acceptance that closes it and the observation that falsifies it.
`journey.md`'s dated census lines and owed list were corrected in place.

## The roadmap timer's definition and its recovery are tracked

`docs/ROADMAP.md` has been mirrored onto the Roadmap Test canvas by a user timer
since 10 September. Everything that defines that timer — the 274-line `sync.mjs`,
its README, the two systemd units, the published-package dependency — lived only
in one machine's home directory, outside every repository. Nothing about it could
be reviewed, backed up, or handed to a second person.

That cost eight days. On 10 September the machine re-badged; the timer's claim
(`cron:roadmap-sync`) stayed on the home's desk, on a badge nothing held any more.
The refusal the script printed on every fire advised
`isocan identity --name "Your Name" --session` — which would have minted a second
`Roadmap Sync`, either refused as name-taken or admitted and splitting every later
version and every per-actor `undo` across two actors. Nobody followed it, and the
canvas simply stopped moving while `origin/main` moved 98 commits past the commit
the canvas named.

The recovery was run for real today: the same session key, `--as` rather than
`--name`, which the desk deliberately excludes from “held elsewhere”, so no vouch
was needed. The timer's own next scheduled fire landed 109 operations in 90
seconds, every one stamped with the actor it resumed and none anonymous; the fire
after that was quiet in two seconds. The four steps, the trap and the verification
— read off the persisted canvas rather than the script's own log — are in
[`docs/projects/roadmap-canvas/runbook.md`](../projects/roadmap-canvas/runbook.md),
with the timer's definition of record beside it under
[`roadmap-sync/`](../projects/roadmap-canvas/roadmap-sync/): the script, both
units, the package manifest and the operational README, which now carries the
recovery section the live copy lacks.

The wrong advice at its source is fixed separately: `packages/api/src/connect.ts`
now asks the home for the single session key the caller stated and prints
`--as <actorId>` when that key is claimed on a badge nobody holds, degrading to
the old generic gesture when the home cannot answer (`isocan-irs`, in review).
Until that reaches the published release the timer consumes, the runbook's first
step — `whoami` under the timer's own key, which already names the stranded actor
— is the way an operator finds the id.

The plan the timer is the manual form of — journey, design and phases for the
roadmap canvas itself — is still owed by its epic; the directory now holds the
operating half that had been living nowhere.
